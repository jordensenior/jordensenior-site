# The Cleanroom and the Darkroom

A few weeks ago, in the mountains above Trento, someone I'd met briefly at an event in London pulled me aside between sessions. "I've been thinking about what you said at NPL," he told me. "About the darkroom. About how it's the same thing." He's a metrologist — his world is standards, uncertainty budgets, the kind of rigour that makes the kilogram what it is. Not someone you'd expect to be moved by a conversation about art. But something in that panel discussion had landed, and it had stayed with him for months.

I keep having versions of this conversation. At the National Physical Laboratory last November, I sat on a panel called *Quantum meets creativity: art, music and the quantum imagination* — the last session of a day otherwise filled with talks on SI units, quantum sensing, and national measurement infrastructure. I was there as a quantum physicist who also spends a significant part of their life in a darkroom, working with silver halide emulsions, nineteenth-century chemistry, and light. The other panellists were Ruth Jarmen and Joseph Gerhardt of Semiconductor Films, artists whose work engages directly with scientific data and natural phenomena. Ilana Wisby moderated. The room was still full at 5pm, which in conference terms is a minor miracle.

What I said — what I keep trying to articulate, and what this essay is my attempt to finally write down properly — is that the creative practice I have in the darkroom and the experimental practice I was trained in as a physicist are not analogous. They are not metaphors for each other. They are the same thing.

## Hands

I trained as an experimental physicist working with superconducting circuits. For years, my daily life involved cleanroom fabrication: depositing thin films of aluminium and niobium, patterning Josephson junctions through electron-beam lithography, etching, oxidising, measuring. You learn the materials through sustained engagement. You develop an intuition for how a particular evaporator behaves — not through guesswork, but through the accumulation of thousands of observations, most of which never make it into a lab notebook. You learn to read the colour of a thin film by eye and know, before any instrument confirms it, whether the deposition went well. This isn't mystical. It's pattern recognition, trained over years, operating below the threshold of conscious reasoning.

This is the part of science that non-scientists rarely hear about. The public narrative is all theory, equations, breakthroughs — the clean, cerebral version. But the practice of experimental physics is *craft*. It is manual, iterative, and deeply physical. You are wrestling with matter, trying to coax it into doing something that requires extraordinary precision. You fail often. You learn to diagnose failure quickly. And the skill of the experimentalist lies not in avoiding the unexpected, but in recognising when something unexpected is worth pursuing — while maintaining the rigour to distinguish a genuine finding from noise.

As my career progressed — as happens in science — I moved from the bench to management. I lead a research team now, working on quantum computing hardware at VTT in Finland. It is good, important work, and I believe in it. But I am no longer the one in the cleanroom. I am no longer the one with my hands on the materials. And I felt that loss acutely, in a way that surprised me.

The darkroom filled the gap. Not as a hobby, not as relaxation — as a *practice*. The same practice, transplanted into a different medium.

## Lith

Let me get specific. Lith printing is a darkroom technique where you massively overexpose a sheet of photographic paper and then develop it in heavily diluted lith developer — sometimes 1:20 or beyond. At these extreme dilutions, the chemistry behaves differently from conventional development. Hydroquinone, the developing agent, doesn't oxidise directly to quinone as it normally would. Instead, it passes through a transient intermediate state: the semiquinone radical — a partially oxidised molecule that is itself a far more aggressive developing agent than the hydroquinone that produced it.

In the shadows, where many silver halide crystals begin developing simultaneously, semiquinone accumulates locally. Development accelerates. The more silver reduces, the more semiquinone is generated, and the faster the process runs. This is infectious development: an autocatalytic cascade. Meanwhile, in the highlights, where few crystals are developing, semiquinone concentration stays low. Development proceeds slowly, almost linearly. The result is a print with luminous, delicate highlights alongside gritty, high-contrast shadows — tonal separation that emerges not from contrast filtration but from the chemistry itself.

If you're a physicist, the operational structure is immediately recognisable. You are working at the edge of an instability. You are riding a nonlinear dynamical process, using your eyes and your trained timing — not a feedback controller — to snatch the print from the developer at exactly the right moment on an exponential curve. But this isn't randomness. It's the same kind of skilled intervention you make when tuning a qubit near a transition, or biasing a SQUID just below its critical current. You prepare carefully, you understand the system deeply, and then you execute in a narrow window where your preparation meets the system's behaviour.

Every lith print is different. You cannot reproduce one exactly. But this isn't because you lack control — it's because you are working in a regime where small variations in initial conditions propagate through the autocatalytic cascade. You understand *why* each print differs. The chemistry is legible if you've done the work to learn its language.

## Sabattier

The Sabattier effect is produced by briefly re-exposing a print to light partway through development, then continuing to develop. The remaining unexposed silver halides — those that would normally stay clear — suddenly become exposed. These newly formed silver bodies overpower the original development centres, causing partial tone reversal. A distinct line — the Mackie line — forms at the boundary between reversed and non-reversed regions, an edge effect produced by local depletion of developer where the two zones meet.

The print holds both the positive and the negative simultaneously.

I'm wary of cheap quantum metaphors. The popular press has done enough damage with "Schrödinger's cat" as a stand-in for anything that might be two things at once. But what I find most compelling about the Sabattier effect isn't the superposition analogy — it's the *decision* it encodes. You choose when to re-expose. Too early, and you get a near-complete reversal. Too late, and the effect is barely visible. The moment you choose determines the ratio of positive to negative in the final image, and you cannot undo it.

This is experimental science. Every measurement is an intervention. Every choice of when to read out a qubit, when to apply a pulse, when to terminate a sequence — these are irreversible commitments made under incomplete information. The skill lies in knowing enough about the system to make the commitment at the right moment. The experimenter, like the printer, is embedded in the system. Your actions shape the outcome — not arbitrarily, but through informed judgement.

When I work with the Sabattier effect, I use a second enlarger for the re-exposure. This gives me precise control over both timing and intensity. It's the same instinct I had in the cleanroom: understand the physics well enough that you can design your intervention deliberately, then execute.

## Mordançage

If lith printing is about riding instability and the Sabattier effect is about irreversible commitment, then mordançage is about controlled destruction as a creative act.

The process works like this: you make a conventional black-and-white print, fully developed and fixed. Then you immerse it in a mordançage bath — copper(II) chloride, glacial acetic acid, and hydrogen peroxide. The copper ions oxidise the metallic silver in the darkest areas of the print, while the peroxide regenerates the copper and simultaneously attacks the gelatin matrix. The gelatin in the heavily bleached areas swells, weakens, and lifts away from the paper base. You can peel it back, push it around with a brush or a jet of water, fold it over itself. The shadows literally detach and become sculptural.

Then you redevelop. The remaining silver halide — the parts that weren't originally exposed — now develops to black. What was light becomes dark. What was dark is gone, peeled away or floating. The print is transformed utterly.

You absolutely can plan a mordançage print. You can control your concentrations, your timing, your temperature. You can predict which areas will lift based on silver density. You can develop a working method that produces consistent results session to session. But the process retains an irreducible variability — the gelatin lifts where the molecular bonds are weakest, and those weak points vary with paper batch, chemistry age, and conditions you can influence but not fully dictate. The prints you make are therefore unrepeatable in their specifics, even when your method is disciplined.

In the cleanroom, some of the most important developments come not from happy accidents but from the disciplined practice of pushing processes to their limits and being attentive enough to recognise when the results reveal something unexpected. An etch that undercuts in a way your model didn't predict isn't a lucky break — it's a signal, legible only to someone who understood what the model *should* have predicted. The ability to read that signal is one of the most important skills an experimentalist develops. Mordançage demands the same attentiveness: not passivity in the face of randomness, but active reading of a system that you understand deeply but don't fully control.

## What the darkroom teaches

I want to be precise about the claim I'm making, because it's easy to slide into vague gestures about "creativity" and "science" being "connected." That kind of talk is pleasant but toothless. I'm making a stronger claim.

The practice of experimental science and the practice of analogue photography share the same fundamental structure. Both involve direct physical engagement with materials that have their own properties and resistances. Both require the development of tacit knowledge — embodied skill that can't be fully articulated but is built on deep technical understanding. Both operate at the boundary between control and emergence, where preparation and understanding enable you to work productively with systems whose behaviour you can't fully determine in advance. Both demand comfort with irreversibility, with the fact that you cannot undo your interventions. And both produce results that are unrepeatable in their specifics — no two experimental runs are identical, just as no two darkroom prints are identical, even from the same negative.

This isn't mysticism. It's a description of what it means to work experimentally. The rigour is what makes the emergence meaningful. Without deep understanding of the semiquinone cascade, you can't ride it. Without knowing why gelatin lifts in mordançage, you can't work with the lifting. Without understanding junction physics, you can't tune a qubit. The understanding and the unpredictability aren't in tension — they're inseparable. The understanding is what lets you operate in the regime where interesting things happen.

When I moved away from the bench and into management — when my days became meetings and emails and strategy documents — I didn't stop being an experimentalist. But I lost the context in which that identity could express itself. I lost the physical engagement, the tacit learning, the daily negotiation with materials. The darkroom gave it back.

Not as compensation. Not as therapy. As *continuation*.

## The community

One thing I didn't fully anticipate when I started showing work and talking about this publicly is how many people in science carry a similar tension. Physicists who paint. Engineers who throw ceramics. Mathematicians who play jazz with a seriousness that rivals their research. These aren't weekend diversions. For many of us, they are essential — they are the part of the practice that the institution doesn't accommodate but that the practitioner can't do without.

The International Year of Quantum Science and Technology has created space for these conversations in a way that feels new. The fact that NPL programmed a panel on art and creativity at the end of a day about metrology was significant. It said: this matters. It is part of what we do. And the response — the full room at 5pm, the conversations that followed, the metrologist in the mountains months later still thinking about it — tells me the appetite is real.

I don't think we need to justify art to scientists by proving it makes them "more creative" or "better problem-solvers" in some instrumentalised sense. That framing diminishes both practices. What I want to say instead is simpler: if you are an experimentalist, you already know how to make art. You already have the skills — the patience, the material intuition built on deep understanding, the comfort with failure, the eye for when something unexpected is actually something important. You do it every day in the lab. The only question is whether you also do it somewhere else.

For me, that somewhere else is a darkroom in Helsinki, working with silver, copper, and light — snatching a lith print out of the developer at exactly the moment the shadows start to race. It feels like science. Because it is.
