# Chromatic Archaeology: Reading the Ruins of Colour Photography

A fully processed RA4 print presents itself as a unified surface—a seamless blend of cyan, magenta, and yellow that our eyes read as full colour. We accept this illusion so completely that we forget it's an illusion at all. But what happens when we literally peel back that promise? These mordançage-treated RA4 prints aren't just technical experiments—they're archaeological excavations into the physical structure of colour photography itself. Each lifted layer, each chromatic bubble, each yellow-green chemical burn reveals something fundamental: colour photography has always been a construction, a careful stack of molecular approximations that only coheres when we don't look too closely.

## The Revelation of Structure

When I first saw distinct colour layers separating—cyan peeling away from magenta like old paint from a wall—I realised I was witnessing something typically invisible: the actual architecture of colour photography. Intellectually, we understand that colour prints contain separate dye layers, but seeing them physically delaminate transforms theoretical knowledge into visceral understanding.

It's like those anatomical models where transparent layers reveal muscles, organs, bones. Except here, we're dissecting the anatomy of an image. The cyan layer, responsible for controlling red light, floats free. The magenta layer, modulating green, bubbles up. The yellow, so often invisible in its subtlety, suddenly appears as distinct clouds of colour.

The visible stratification exposes the fundamental arbitrariness of photographic colour. There's nothing natural about dividing the visible spectrum into three dye layers. It's a compromise, an engineering solution that works well enough that we've forgotten it's a compromise at all. These mordançage prints make that compromise visible again.


## Reading the Ruins

Looking at these prints requires developing new visual literacy. We can't read them as conventional photographs—representations of something that existed in front of the camera. Instead, we read them as palimpsests, as geological cross-sections, as maps of chemical events.

The lifted areas become negative spaces that reveal the polyethylene base—photography's hidden substrate suddenly visible. The colour separations show the arbitrary divisions we use to construct "natural" colour. The chemical burns mark time's passage not in years but in minutes of reaction.

This is photography that documents its own making and unmaking simultaneously. The image shows both what the camera captured and what the chemistry destroyed. Past and future collapse into a present that won't hold still.

What colours emerge only through destruction?



## Time as a Dimension

Traditional photography freezes time—that's its fundamental promise. These mordançage prints reject that promise entirely. They exist in continuous transformation, their chemistry still active, their colours still shifting. A print photographed today will look different tomorrow. The trapped chemistry continues its work, creating aureoles of colour that expand like slow explosions.

The temporal dimension transforms them from photographs into performances. The image isn't the final print—it's the entire process of transformation. Watching a print change over days or weeks becomes part of experiencing the work. We're not just viewing an image; we're witnessing its ongoing decay.

This recalls the Japanese concept of *mono no aware*—the pathos of things, the gentle sadness of transient beauty. These prints embody that aesthetic philosophy in chemical form. Their beauty is inseparable from their impermanence. They're more beautiful because they're dying.

## The Honesty of Instability

There's something fundamentally honest about prints that refuse to stabilise. All colour photographs fade—that's been true since the earliest chromogenic processes. Even the most archival contemporary materials will eventually succumb to entropy. These mordançage prints simply acknowledge that fate more openly, even celebrate it.

<figure class="inline end" markdown>
    <img src="/experiments/012-Ra4Mordancage/ra_3c.jpeg" align="right" width="275px" style="margin:10px;" alt="Gallery" />
</figure> 

Consider the history of colour photography's false promises of permanence. Kodacolor prints from the 1940s have shifted dramatically. Ektachromes from various eras showed poor dark fading and colour shifts. Even modern RA4 prints, despite manufacturer claims, show measurable colour shifts within decades. Colour photography has chased archival stability for over a century and consistently struggled to achieve it.

These deliberately unstable prints suggest a different approach: embrace the impermanence. Make work that acknowledges its own mortality. Create images that perform their own decay. There's more truth in a print that openly deteriorates than in one that pretends to permanence it can't actually achieve.

## Chemical Terroir

Each batch of prints develops its own character based on subtle variables—the exact temperature that day, the chemistry's age, the specific paper batch, even the humidity in the darkroom. Like wine reflecting its terroir, these prints carry the signature of their specific moment of creation. This chemical variability builds on the kinetic darkroom experiments where movement during enlargement created unique prints—but here the uniqueness comes not from exposure technique but from chemical chaos itself.

This irreproducibility runs counter to photography's supposed democratic reproducibility. An entire medium has been built on the premise that prints can be repeated, that editions can be matched. These prints reject that industrial logic. Each one is necessarily unique, carrying the chemical fingerprint of its specific moment of destruction.

The yellow-green pollution varies from print to print—sometimes spreading in circular aureoles, sometimes in directional streams, sometimes in explosive bursts. These patterns can't be controlled, only influenced. The chemistry has its own agency, its own aesthetic decisions. The photographer becomes collaborator rather than author.

## Living With Instability

These prints present practical challenges that become part of their conceptual framework. Storage becomes performance—do I arrest the chemical reactions through extensive washing, or let them continue? Display becomes documentation—each viewing captures a specific moment in an ongoing transformation. Archiving becomes paradox—how do you preserve something designed to decay?

<br>

So far, I've chosen to let the chemistry continue working. Some prints I've washed thoroughly after initial documentation, effectively freezing them at a particular stage of degradation. Others remain unwashed, their evolution continuing in storage. After several weeks, the most aggressive reactions slow, though subtle shifts in colour and density continue for months.

The fragility matters too. These prints can't be handled carelessly—lifted emulsion layers remain delicate, prone to further tearing or complete detachment. They exist in gallery spaces only with significant care, protected from touch, humidity extremes, and direct light. Their physical vulnerability reinforces their conceptual stance: impermanence made manifest.

## Digital Mediation and Material Reality

Ironically, these supremely physical, unmistakably analogue prints often reach viewers as digital files. The photographs you're seeing of these prints are digital captures of analogue destructions. There's something poetic about using digital photography's stability to document analogue photography's instability.

But the digital documentation is necessarily incomplete. It can't capture the dimensionality—the actual lifting of emulsion creates topographical variations that shift with viewing angle. It can't capture the ongoing transformation—each digital capture freezes what refuses to be frozen. It can't capture the chemical smell, the tackiness of incompletely dried emulsion, the fragility of layers barely holding together.

This gap between digital documentation and physical reality emphasises what we lose in photography's digital migration. These prints insist on their materiality in ways that can't be fully digitised. They're photographs that resist photography.

## Toward a New Colour Aesthetic

These experiments suggest possibilities for a new relationship with colour photography—one that acknowledges construction rather than assuming naturalism, embraces instability rather than pursuing permanence, celebrates accident alongside intention.

Imagine exhibitions where prints continue evolving throughout the show's run. Opening night viewers see different works than closing night viewers. The exhibition becomes durational, performative, unrepeatable.

Imagine editions where each print is deliberately unique—not through numbering but through chemical variation. The same negative yields infinite variations, each carrying its own chemical signature.

Imagine hybrid processes where controlled sections undergo mordançage whilst others remain stable, creating images that exist partially in transformation, partially in stasis. Time moves differently across the picture plane.


## Chemistry as Revelation

These RA4 mordançage experiments aren't trying to solve a problem—archival instability isn't a bug here but a feature. They're not trying to improve colour photography—improvement implies we agree on what colour photography should do. Instead, they're revealing what colour photography is: a precarious construction that has been naturalised through repetition and convention.

By forcing colour materials into conditions they weren't designed to survive, we learn something essential about their nature. By watching them fail, we understand how they succeed. By seeing them come apart, we grasp how they're put together.

Standing in the darkroom, watching colours separate and transform, smelling the acidic bite of chemistry doing things it shouldn't, I'm reminded that experimental futures might not lie in new technologies but in deliberately misusing existing ones. Every established process contains the seeds of its own subversion. Every stable system can be destabilised to reveal new truths.

The twenty-first century has given us perfect digital colour, infinite reproducibility, absolute control. Perhaps what we need now is imperfect chemical colour, radical uniqueness, collaborative chaos. Perhaps we need photographs that admit what all photographs actually are: unstable negotiations between light and time, chemistry and chance, intention and accident.

The yellow-green stains spreading across these prints aren't failures—they're revelations. They show us colour photography with its mask removed, beautiful in its honesty about its own constructed nature. They remind us that every photograph, no matter how stable it appears, is actually a slow explosion of chemistry barely held in check.
